# Chapter 9 — Pricing (concept inventory / ground truth)

## Slide-emphasized points (instructor)
- Price: the money or other considerations, including other goods and services, exchanged for the ownership or use of a product. "The three [other] marketing mix elements go a long way to create value — pricing is the attempt to capture that value."
- Importance of price — pricing has a dual purpose/role: (1) generating revenue; (2) learning about the marketplace (e.g., the quality heuristic — consumers use price to judge quality).
- Important points: poor pricing strategies can make reasonable marketing strategies financially ineffective; pricing is the "moment of truth" — where the focus of a transaction eventually comes to.
- Price as an indicator of value: Value pricing — increasing product or service benefits while maintaining or decreasing price.
- Consumer psychology and pricing: behaviour changes (what do you want consumers to do?); reference prices (what we use to compare purchase prices); price endings (odd/even prices — why used? perception of bargain vs quality).
- Comparison: State Farm positions on "relationship"; Allstate positions on "price" ($278 saved).
- Three key considerations (3 Cs) when studying pricing: 1 Customers, 2 Costs, 3 Competition ("Your price" sits among "your costs," "competitor's price," and "what customer wants to pay").
- Figure 9-2 Four approaches for selecting an approximate price level (MEMORIZE buckets):
  - Demand-oriented: skimming, penetration, prestige, odd-even, target, bundle, yield management.
  - Cost-oriented: standard markup, cost-plus.
  - Profit-oriented: target profit, target return on sales, target return on investment.
  - Competition-oriented: customary, above/at/below market, loss leader.
- Demand-oriented approaches emphasize factors underlying expected customer tastes and preferences (Nintendo Wii U = penetration; airline seats = yield management).
- Cost-oriented: wholesalers and retailers often express markup as a percentage of PRICE (Figure 9-3: selling price $75 = 100%; cost $60 = 80%; markup $15 = 20% — "price is always 100 percent when markup is relative to price"). Manufacturers often express markup as a percentage of COST (markup $15 / cost $60 = 25%).
- Profit-oriented: set a target of a specific dollar volume of profit, or a percentage of sales or investment; depends on accurate estimates of demand.
- Competition-oriented: rather than emphasize demand, cost, or profit, the focus is on what competitors are doing (iPhone 17 = above market; milk = loss leader).
- Cost-based vs value-based pricing (Nagle/Holden): cost-based = Product -> Cost -> Price -> Value -> Customers; value-based = Customers -> Value -> Price -> Cost -> Product.
- Estimating demand and revenue: creating the correct price begins the forecasting process; with a price known, determine extent of customer demand (marketing efforts, competitor efforts); translate to estimated revenue. Forecasting methods: qualitative, regression, multiple regression, time-series.
- Break-even analysis (Figure 9-8, picture frame store): BEP(quantity) = Fixed cost / (Unit price − Unit variable cost). Total revenue line vs total cost line (fixed + variable); profit above BEP, loss below.
- Pricing objectives (specify the role of price in an organization's marketing and strategic plans): Profit (long-run, current, target return), Sales, Market share, Volume, Survival, Social responsibility.
- Pricing constraints (factors that limit the range of prices): demand for the product class, product, and brand; newness of the product / stage in the PLC; cost of producing and marketing the product; competitors' prices (textbook also: single product vs product line; type of competitive market).
- Legal and ethical: Price fixing — competitors collaborate/conspire to set prices; Price discrimination — different customers get different prices (illegal when it lessens competition / discriminates among competing buyers); Deceptive pricing — price offers that mislead; Predatory pricing — low price set to drive competitors out of business.
- Setting a final price (4 steps): Step 1 Select an approximate price level (one of the 4 approaches). Step 2 Set the list or quoted price — one-price policy vs flexible-price policy. Step 3 Make special adjustments to the list or quoted price — Discounts (reductions from list price given as a reward for some favourable activity): quantity, seasonal, trade (functional), cash; Allowances (trade-in, promotional); Geographic adjustments (price reflects transportation component): FOB origin pricing ("free on board"), uniform delivered pricing; Dynamic pricing (Uber surge). Step 4 Monitor and adjust prices.

## Core terms (textbook)
- Price equation: Final price = List price − Incentives and allowances + Extra fees. (Dishwasher: $800 − ($200 discount + $20 trade-in) + $30 financing = $610.)
- Barter: exchanging goods/services for other goods/services rather than money.
- Value = perceived benefits / price. Raising benefits or lowering price raises value. Price can signal quality (quality heuristic): for some products a low price implies poor quality and poor perceived value (diamonds).
- Value pricing: increasing benefits while maintaining or lowering price.
- Profit equation: Profit = Total revenue − Total cost = (Unit price × Quantity sold) − (Fixed cost + Unit variable cost × Quantity).
- Demand curve: graph relating quantity sold and price; demand factors: consumer tastes, price and availability of substitutes, consumer income. Movement along the curve = price change; shift of the curve = change in demand factors.
- Price elasticity of demand: % change in quantity demanded / % change in price. Elastic (>1): quantity very responsive to price (luxuries, many substitutes) -> lowering price raises total revenue. Inelastic (<1): quantity not very responsive (necessities, few substitutes, gasoline, cigarettes) -> raising price raises total revenue.
- Total revenue = P × Q; average revenue = TR/Q; marginal revenue = change in TR from selling one more unit.
- Costs: total cost = fixed cost + variable cost. Fixed costs do not change with quantity (rent, salaries, insurance); variable costs change directly with quantity (materials, labour per unit); unit variable cost = VC/Q; marginal cost = change in TC from producing one more unit.
- Break-even analysis: technique examining the relationship between total revenue and total cost to determine profitability at various output levels. BEP quantity = FC / (P − UVC). Break-even chart.
- Markup: on selling price = (price − cost)/price; on cost = (price − cost)/cost. Standard markup pricing: adding a fixed percentage to the cost of all items in a class (retailers; also manufacturers adding X% to total costs = standard markup). Cost-plus pricing: summing total unit cost and adding a specific amount/percentage (common in construction, government contracts, services).
- Demand-oriented approaches: Skimming — high initial price for a new product to recover R&D; works when enough customers are price-insensitive (inelastic demand segment), high price signals quality, competitors can't enter quickly, production costs don't fall fast with volume. Penetration — low initial price to gain mass market quickly; works when many buyers are price-sensitive (elastic), economies of scale/experience lower unit costs, low price discourages competitors. Prestige — high price so status-conscious consumers will buy (demand curve bends backward; lowering price can reduce demand). Odd-even — prices set a few dollars/cents under an even number ($499.99) to suggest a bargain; even prices suggest quality. Target pricing — estimate the price consumers are willing to pay, then work backward to design/cost the product (deliberately adjusting composition/features). Bundle pricing — marketing two or more products in a single package price (value meal, cable+internet). Yield management — charging different prices to maximize revenue for a set amount of capacity at a given time (airlines, hotels, rideshare peak pricing).
- Profit-oriented: Target profit pricing — set price to achieve a specific annual dollar profit (Charlie Tuna: 42,000 = P×21,000 − [410,000 + 40×21,000] -> P = $61.52). Target return-on-sales pricing — price to achieve a profit that is a specified percentage of sales volume. Target return-on-investment pricing — price to achieve an annual target ROI percentage.
- Competition-oriented: Customary pricing — price set by tradition/standardized channel/other competitive factors (candy bars, vending items). Above-, at-, or below-market pricing — using the "market price" as a benchmark (luxury = above; Walmart = below). Loss-leader pricing — deliberately selling a product below its customary price to attract attention and traffic in hopes shoppers buy other products (milk, eggs, Black Friday TVs).
- Pricing objectives detail: profit (managing for long-run profit, current profit, target return); sales (dollar/unit sales); market share (ratio of sales revenue to total industry sales); volume (unit volume; maintain capacity); survival (short-run, cover costs); social responsibility (recognize obligations to customers and society, e.g., pricing medicines affordably).
- Pricing constraints detail: demand for the product class/product/brand; newness of product/PLC stage (new products can price higher); single product vs product line (cannibalization and complementary effects); cost of producing and marketing (in the long run, costs set a FLOOR under price; in the short run a firm may price below full cost but not indefinitely); cost of changing prices and time period they apply; type of competitive market — pure monopoly (one seller, lots of pricing latitude), oligopoly (few sellers, price wars), monopolistic competition (many sellers, some differentiation), pure competition (many sellers, identical products, price = market); competitors' prices.
- Setting the list/quoted price: one-price policy (fixed price; same price to all buyers — Saturn cars, Walmart) vs flexible-price policy (dynamic pricing; different prices depending on individual buyers and purchase situations — car dealers, transit fares by age/employment).
- Discounts: Quantity discounts — reductions for buying larger quantities (noncumulative: single order; cumulative: over a period, encourages loyalty). Seasonal discounts — encourage buyers to stock inventory earlier than normal demand requires (lawnmowers in winter, beer pre-Canada Day). Trade (functional) discounts — given to resellers (wholesalers/retailers) for performing marketing functions, quoted as a chain (e.g., 30/10/5 off list). Cash discounts — reward for paying bills promptly (2/10 net 30 = 2% off if paid within 10 days, full due in 30).
- Allowances: trade-in allowance (price reduction for turning in an old item), promotional allowances (cash or free goods to resellers for advertising/promoting the product; must be offered proportionally to all competing resellers under the Competition Act).
- Geographic adjustments: FOB origin pricing — "free on board" the vehicle at the seller's location; title passes to the buyer at the point of loading; buyer pays freight. Uniform delivered pricing — price includes all transportation costs; seller pays shipping; variations: single-zone (postage stamp) pricing, multiple-zone pricing, FOB with freight-allowed (freight absorption), basing-point pricing.
- Dynamic pricing: adjusting prices in real time based on demand (Uber surge).
- Legal/ethical detail (Competition Act, Competition Bureau): price fixing — horizontal (among competitors) or vertical (manufacturer-retailer resale price maintenance); price discrimination — charging different prices to different competing buyers for like quantities (illegal when it lessens competition); deceptive pricing — bait and switch, bargains conditional on other purchases, comparable value comparisons, comparisons with suggested prices, double ticketing (must sell at the lower price); predatory pricing — charging a very low price to injure/drive out competitors, then raising prices.

## Traps / confusables
- Skimming (high initial; inelastic; recover R&D) vs penetration (low initial; elastic; scale) vs prestige (high price for status; permanently) vs above-market pricing (competition-oriented benchmark).
- Standard markup (percent added to cost of all items in a class; retailers/manufacturers) vs cost-plus (unit cost + specific amount; contracts) vs target profit (specific dollar profit) vs target return on sales/investment.
- Markup on price vs markup on cost (same $15 markup = 20% of price or 25% of cost).
- One-price vs flexible-price policy; flexible = dynamic = different prices for different buyers/situations.
- Quantity vs seasonal vs trade/functional vs cash discounts; trade-in vs promotional allowances.
- FOB origin (buyer pays freight; title at loading) vs uniform delivered (seller pays; price includes freight).
- Price fixing (conspiring competitors) vs price discrimination (different prices to competing buyers) vs predatory (below cost to kill rivals) vs deceptive (misleading offers; bait-and-switch, double ticketing).
- Loss leader (below customary price to drive traffic) vs penetration (low introductory price for a NEW product) vs below-market pricing (ongoing positioning).
- Yield management (capacity-based time pricing) vs flexible pricing (buyer-based) vs bundle pricing.
- Pricing objectives (what price should achieve) vs pricing constraints (what limits price).
- Elastic (TR rises when price falls) vs inelastic (TR rises when price rises).
- BEP = FC/(P − UVC); the 4 steps to set a final price: approximate level -> list price -> adjustments -> monitor.
