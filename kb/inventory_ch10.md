# Chapter 10 — Marketing Channels and Supply Chains (concept inventory / ground truth)

## Slide-emphasized points (instructor)
- Reaching potential buyers is an essential part of successful marketing; buyers benefit from well-structured and efficient distribution systems; channels can be direct and indirect.
- Instructor's "two key tradeoffs": channel decisions require considering two elements — COST and LEVEL OF SERVICE. "What must every channel partner provide?" -> value (each intermediary must add value/perform functions more efficiently than the producer could).
- Marketing channel: consists of individuals and firms involved in the process of making a product or service available for use or consumption by consumers or industrial users. Intermediaries: individuals or firms performing a role in the marketing channel, involved in making a product available.
- Figure 10-1 Terms used for marketing intermediaries: Middleman = another name for intermediary. Agent or broker = any intermediary with legal authority to act on behalf of another channel member (e.g., a manufacturer); does NOT take title. Wholesaler = any intermediary who sells to other intermediaries, usually to retailers; term usually applies to consumer goods. Retailer = an intermediary who sells to consumers. Distributor = a general term describing intermediaries who perform a variety of functions (selling, maintaining inventories, extending credit, etc.); usually used for business markets. Dealer = a general term that can mean the same as distributor, retailer, or wholesaler.
- Figure 10-2 How intermediaries minimize transactions: 4 producers × 4 buyers with no intermediary = 16 contacts; with one intermediary (Best Buy) = 4 + 4 = 8 contacts.
- Figure 10-3 Channel functions performed by intermediaries (MEMORIZE): Transactional — buying (purchasing products for resale), selling (contacting potential customers, promoting products, seeking orders), risk-taking (assuming business risks in the ownership of inventory). Logistical — selection/assorting (putting together a selection of products from several sources), storing (assembling and protecting products at a convenient location), sorting (purchasing in large quantities and dividing into smaller amounts = bulk breaking), transporting (physically moving a product to customers). Facilitating — financing (extending credit to customers), marketing information and research (providing information to customers and suppliers, incl. competitive conditions and trends), also grading.
- Consumer (B2C) channel structures (Figure): A Producer -> Consumer (direct: Mary Kay, Dell); B Producer -> Retailer -> Consumer (large retailers: Walmart, Canadian Tire); C Producer -> Wholesaler -> Retailer -> Consumer (most common for low-cost, low-unit-value items: candy, magazines); D Producer -> Agent -> Wholesaler -> Retailer -> Consumer (agents bring producers and wholesalers together; Mars candy).
- Business (B2B) channel structures: A Producer -> Industrial user (direct: IBM); B Producer -> Industrial distributor -> Industrial user (Caterpillar); C Producer -> Agent -> Industrial user; D Producer -> Agent -> Industrial distributor -> Industrial user. Agents do not take physical possession of the goods.
- Dual distribution: a firm reaches buyers by using two or more different types of channels for the same basic product (e.g., selling through retailers AND its own website). Websites can play multiple roles (transactional, promotional). Strategic channel alliances: one firm's marketing channel is used to sell another firm's products (Starbucks coffee sold in Safeway).
- Multichannel marketing: the blending of different communication and delivery channels that are mutually reinforcing in attracting, retaining, and building relationships with consumers; leverage the value-adding capabilities of each. Marketplace = face-to-face; Marketspace = face-to-screen (online). Omni-channel = seamless experience across all channels.
- Global channel strategy (Figure 10-7): Seller -> Seller's international marketing headquarters -> Channels between nations -> Channels within foreign nations -> Final consumer; sophistication increases as a country's economic infrastructure develops.
- Vertical marketing systems (VMS): professionally managed and centrally coordinated marketing channels designed to achieve channel economies and maximum marketing impact. Types (Figure): Corporate VMS (Ralph Lauren/Polo — ownership of successive stages; forward integration = producer owns retail; backward integration = retailer owns manufacturing/wholesaling). Contractual VMS (Wholesaler-sponsored voluntary chains — IGA; Retailer-sponsored cooperatives — Home Hardware; Franchise programs — four types: Manufacturer-sponsored retail franchise system (Ford dealers), Manufacturer-sponsored wholesale franchise system (Coca-Cola bottlers), Service-sponsored retail franchise system (Canadian Tire, H&R Block), Service-sponsored franchise system (Holiday Inn, McDonald's). Administered VMS (Procter & Gamble — coordination through the size and influence of one channel member rather than ownership or contracts).
- Factors affecting channel choice (Figure): MARKET — geographic concentration, number of potential customers, type of market (consumer vs business), order size. PRODUCT — technical factors (complex -> direct), perishability (-> short/direct), unit value (low -> long/indirect), product life cycle stage. COMPANY — financial resources and ability of management, desire for channel control.
- Channel design considerations (3): (1) Target market coverage — intensive (as many outlets as possible; convenience goods), exclusive (only one retailer in a geographic area; specialty goods: luxury cars, yachts, fragrances), or selective (a few retailers in an area; shopping goods: appliances, apparel). (2) Satisfying buyer requirements — information, convenience, variety, pre- and/or post-sale services. (3) Profitability — distribution, advertising, and selling expenses vs margins.
- Motivating channel members — channel power sources: coercive (threat of punishment/withdrawal), reward (incentives), legitimate (authority from position/contract), others (expert, referent). Conflicts are possible (e.g., disintermediation). Channel conflict: when one channel member believes another is engaged in behaviour that prevents it from achieving its goals. Vertical conflict = between different levels (manufacturer vs wholesaler/retailer; e.g., disintermediation — producer bypasses an intermediary and sells direct; or a producer squeezing margins). Horizontal conflict = between intermediaries at the same level (two dealers of the same brand; dual distribution causing a retailer to compete with the maker's online store).
- Channel captain: a channel member who coordinates, directs, and supports other members (can be a producer, wholesaler, or retailer, e.g., Walmart).
- Evaluating channel members: sales, inventory levels, treatment of damaged/lost goods, others (customer service, cooperation in promotion).
- E-commerce trends (hostinger source): top categories fashion and electronics; use of AI (personalization), AR, VR; CRM/customer support important; hybrid commerce; multiple payment options (buy now, pay later); voice activation; social selling; data privacy; sustainability; fast, free delivery.
- Logistics: activities that focus on getting the right amount of the right products to the right place at the right time at the lowest possible cost; built around the expected level of customer service; requires collaboration, coordination, information sharing among manufacturers, suppliers, distributors — seamless.
- Supply chain management: the integration and organization of information and logistics activities across firms in a supply chain for the purpose of creating and delivering goods and services that provide value to consumers; need to harmonize the supply chain with the marketing strategy.
- Figure 10-9 Supply chain vs marketing channel: Suppliers -> Producer -> Consumers. Supplier network (all parties supplying raw materials and parts) + Marketing channel (all parties involved in distributing and promoting the product). Logistics management = coordinates the movement of goods throughout; Supply chain management = integrates information and logistics across all parties.
- Key logistics functions in a supply chain (Figure): Transportation (criteria: cost, time, capability, dependability, accessibility, frequency; modes: rail, truck, air, water, pipeline, intermodal). Order processing (electronic data interchange — EDI). Inventory management (just-in-time — JIT). Warehousing (public or private; traditional storage warehouses vs distribution centres/cross-docking; combination).

## Core terms (textbook)
- Direct channel: producer and ultimate consumer deal directly with each other; no intermediaries. Indirect channel: intermediaries are inserted between producer and consumers and perform numerous channel functions.
- Utilities created by intermediaries: time, place, form, possession.
- Electronic marketing channels: channels that use the Internet to make goods and services available (e.g., Amazon, Expedia). Direct marketing channels: allow consumers to buy by interacting with advertising media without a face-to-face meeting (catalogues, telemarketing, direct mail, TV home shopping, online).
- Industrial distributor: performs functions similar to a wholesaler in business markets (sells to industrial users).
- Multichannel marketing vs omni-channel retailing (seamless experience among all shopping channels — online, catalogue, in-store).
- Forward integration (producer owns intermediaries downstream, e.g., Apple Stores) vs backward integration (retailer/wholesaler owns upstream production — a coffee chain owning the bakery that supplies its cookies).
- Horizontal marketing system: two or more unrelated companies at the same level pool resources (distractor term).
- Intensive / selective / exclusive distribution by product type: convenience -> intensive; shopping -> selective; specialty -> exclusive.
- Disintermediation: a channel conflict that arises when a channel member bypasses another member and sells or buys directly (manufacturer selling direct online bypassing retailers).
- Channel power sources: coercive, reward, legitimate, expert, referent.
- Customer service (logistics) concepts: time (order cycle/lead time), dependability, communication, convenience. Total logistics cost: expenses associated with transportation, materials handling and warehousing, inventory, stockouts, order processing, and return goods handling; trade-offs (e.g., faster shipping raises transport cost but lowers inventory cost).
- Inventory reasons: buffer against variations in supply and demand; better service; promote efficiency; speculation. JIT: an inventory supply system that operates with very low inventories and requires fast, on-time delivery; suppliers deliver less merchandise more frequently.
- Vendor-managed inventory (VMI): the supplier determines the product amount and assortment a customer needs and automatically delivers.
- Warehousing: storage warehouses (hold goods for long periods) vs distribution centres (designed to facilitate timely movement; cross-docking = unloading inbound and loading outbound with little or no storage).
- Reverse logistics: a process of reclaiming recyclable and reusable materials, returns, and reworks from the point of consumption or use for repair, remanufacturing, redistribution, or disposal (H&M taking back used clothing to recycle).
- Efficient consumer response (ECR) / quick response (QR): inventory management systems designed to reduce the retailer's lead time for receiving merchandise, lowering inventory investment and improving service.
- Third-party logistics providers (3PL): firms that perform most or all logistics functions for other firms.

## Instructor practice questions (official, with answers)
1. Channel design for specialty products (high-end sports vehicles, exclusive jewellery, yachts) with one retail outlet per area = EXCLUSIVE distribution.
2. Suppliers supply less merchandise on a more frequent basis = JUST-IN-TIME inventory system.
3. Supermarket buys fruits/vegetables from different sources, transfers to stores, arranges for customers = LOGISTICAL function.
4. Omni-channel retailing = the concept of a seamless shopping experience among all shopping channels.
5. Coffee shop chain owns a subsidiary that produces cookies for its stores = BACKWARD INTEGRATION.
6. One channel member believes another's behaviour prevents it from achieving its goals = CHANNEL CONFLICT.
7. Energy drink brand disagrees with one of its major wholesalers over price = VERTICAL CONFLICT.
8. Best example of reverse logistics: H&M accepts used clothing and recycles it into new textiles.
9. Product category most likely to use exclusive distribution = SPECIALTY products.
10. Using intermediaries like wholesalers and retailers = INDIRECT distribution strategy.

## Traps / confusables
- Transactional (buy/sell/risk-take) vs logistical (assort/store/sort/transport) vs facilitating (finance/information/grading) functions.
- Agent/broker (no title) vs wholesaler (sells to other intermediaries; consumer goods) vs distributor (business markets, many functions) vs dealer (generic).
- Dual distribution (same firm, two channel TYPES) vs multichannel marketing (integrated communication + delivery channels) vs strategic channel alliance (one firm uses another's channel) vs omni-channel (seamless).
- Corporate VMS (ownership) vs contractual VMS (contracts: voluntary chains, retailer co-ops, franchises) vs administered VMS (power/size, e.g., P&G, Walmart).
- Forward vs backward integration.
- Intensive (convenience) vs selective (shopping) vs exclusive (specialty).
- Vertical conflict (different levels) vs horizontal conflict (same level) vs disintermediation (bypassing a member).
- Logistics (flow of goods within) vs supply chain management (integration across firms) vs marketing channel (parties distributing/promoting the product).
- JIT (low inventory, frequent deliveries) vs VMI (supplier decides and replenishes) vs cross-docking (warehousing without storage) vs EDI (order processing technology).
- Market factors (geographic concentration, number of customers, type of market, order size) vs product factors (technical, perishability, unit value, PLC) vs company factors (resources, control).
